# Art direction for illustrated 2.5D motion

Use this before choosing assets or arranging shots; adapt to the current brief.

## Establish a project style

Choose an actual reference for each relevant role: overall illustration family,
material/detail level, typography, depth, mechanism motion and optional ending.
Record the scope of each reference rather than adopting every feature globally.

Set a small palette with functional roles: background, main subject, secondary
subject, emphasis, shadow and text. Choose lighting direction and a restrained
surface treatment. Keep line weight, silhouette simplification, depth edges and
texture frequency compatible across foreground, subject and background. A highly
rendered character beside flat diagram parts usually looks assembled.

Keep reusable brand choices separate from scene decisions:
- Fixed within a series: identity assets, exact fonts, color roles, type emphasis,
  material family, characteristic transition/motion rhythm and sound signature.
- Adjustable: camera/viewpoint, staging, scale, number of layers, lighting
  intensity, accent allocation and shot lengths.
- Project-specific: subject, causal mechanism, setting, actors, narration,
  instruments and ending purpose.

Preserve a level the user already likes. More grain, gloss, facets and tiny
surface details are not an automatic improvement. If introducing a different
material family, show a comparable sample before propagating it through a series.

## Give each shot work to do

Describe the new fact and the action that makes it visible. Show an actor changing
position, deformation, state, flow, rotation, scale or relation to another actor.
A whole-frame zoom, animated title or decorative particle drift does not explain
a mechanism by itself.

Choose framing for the information: establish the environment; reveal a hidden
section; follow a transformation; compare states; show the resulting effect.
Change composition when the new fact needs it. Do not convert a complete story
into one static canvas with sequential callouts. Purposeful returns can reinforce
a result when something meaningful has changed.

For distributed processes, show a readable population with variation in depth,
speed, scale or state. Then isolate a sample if that helps explain the change.
A single object against an empty field is useful only when isolation is the point.
Choose counts and activity for clarity; there is no fixed quota.

Compose useful foreground, main and background planes with clear silhouettes,
occlusion and restrained relative motion. Secondary action should reinforce the
main cause: a surface reacts, a gauge responds, a boundary moves, nearby actors
change together. Keep enough quiet space for text and the focal action.

## Maintain continuity and readable scale

Track the same important sample through continuing views. Record anchors,
contact surfaces, crossing times and transformations. A cut may change viewpoint,
but it should not accidentally reset state or move an arrow onto the wrong target.
Use shared events for visible changes and their sound cues.

Keep character size proportional to the phenomenon and other props. A character
is optional; if present, give it a useful response or action and match its detail
level to the scene. Instruments are optional too: draw meaningful tick spacing,
units, ranges and pointer/column motion; preserve the measurement's relationship
to the event rather than animating it independently for decoration.

An optical enlargement needs a clear source region, an arrival at the enlarged
scale, and a stable scale for the event being explained. Keep enlargement,
physical growth and camera travel visually distinguishable.

## Treat typography as artwork

Compare the actual rendered font, weight, scale and spacing with the project
reference, not just a font alias. Use precise, short screen copy separate from
the spoken script. Production directions such as “close-up” are not audience
labels unless explicitly requested as content.

Keep text clear of focal objects and interface crops. Place emphasis lines below
the lowest glyph/dot boundary throughout animation. Test the most difficult word
and smallest labels at phone width. For RTL scripts, check shaping, reading order
and mixed-script handles separately. Align title arrival with the relevant spoken
idea, not simply with the start of a shot.

## Finish on the story's clock

Choose cuts and transitions for continuity or contrast. Camera movement supports
the changing system and should not become the only source of movement. Check
holds in playback; allow time to understand a result, without adding silence to
fill the initial duration estimate.

If a brand ending is requested, design its background and event order explicitly.
Start its entry when the closing invitation needs it, settle the mark, complete
any intentional interaction, and end cleanly after the actual required events.
Do not carry the previous environment behind a logo by accident.

## Inspect what was made

Use the actual encoded film for motion and sound judgments. Still frames can
support layout, type and material inspection; they cannot prove smooth movement
or a good mix. Record technical checks separately from visual preference and
listening. Correct a demonstrated problem, keep one improvement goal per project,
and preserve accepted strengths rather than chasing an arbitrary average score.
